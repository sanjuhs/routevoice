Yeah — this is the RoutingVoice-180 idea we were discussing, and there’s a very clean way to explain it to your friend.

The key sentence is:

The topology of the silicon is the weight.

Instead of building a processor and then storing 15 million weights in SRAM/DRAM, you train the network first, freeze it, ternarize it, and then compile the trained weight matrices into the physical connectivity of the ASIC itself.

RoutingVoice-180: the basic idea

Suppose an ordinary neural-network layer computes

$$ y_i=\sum_j w_{ij}x_j $$

Normally you have something like:

        WEIGHT SRAM
            │
            ▼
x ─────► multiplier ─────► accumulator
         x weight

Every inference involves repeatedly fetching w, moving it to compute, multiplying, accumulating, fetching the next weight, and so on.

Our version deliberately constrains the weights to:

$$ w \in \{-1,0,+1\} $$

This is what you were calling "turnerized" — the usual term is ternarized.

BitNet b1.58 is a real example of this model family: its weights take exactly {-1, 0, +1}, corresponding theoretically to \(\log_2(3)\approx1.58\) bits per weight.

Now look what happens in hardware:

WEIGHT = +1                    WEIGHT = -1
                                             
 x ──────────────► ADD         x ──────────────► SUBTRACT


WEIGHT = 0

 x        NO CONNECTION

So instead of storing:

w[1234] = +1
w[1235] =  0
w[1236] = -1

and reading those values during inference, you manufacture:

x1234 ─────────► + accumulator
x1235             nothing
x1236 ─────────► - accumulator

The existence and destination of the connection encode the parameter.

That is the part I think your friend will find interesting.

Just how much memory disappears?

Take a 15-million-parameter model.

Representation	Weight storage
FP32	60 MB
FP16	30 MB
INT8	15 MB
INT4	7.5 MB
2-bit packed ternary	3.75 MB
Information-theoretic ternary minimum	~2.97 MB
RoutingVoice hardwired weights	0 bytes of weight SRAM/DRAM

And this matters enormously on 180 nm.

A published 180 nm SRAM implementation reported a 6T SRAM cell of about 5.59 µm². At that density, merely the raw bit cells for 30 Mbits — the 2-bit representation of our 15M weights — would occupy about 168 mm², before much of the peripheral circuitry. A historical 16-Mbit 180 nm SRAM demonstration occupied a 207 mm² die.

So trying to make:

15M parameters
×
2-bit SRAM

on an old process is exactly the wrong architecture.

But there is an intermediate possibility: ROM

Because the model never changes, we don't actually need SRAM.

A 0.18 µm via-programmed ROM design demonstrated a 0.749 µm² bit cell, dramatically denser than SRAM.

Thirty million ROM bits at that raw cell density would be roughly:

$$ 30M\times0.749\mu m^2 \approx22.5mm^2 $$

before decoding, sensing, routing and other overhead.

Another SMIC 0.18 µm mask-ROM implementation achieved 16 Kbit in 0.050 mm², demonstrating how much more area-efficient immutable storage can be than conventional SRAM.

So there are really three designs:

  SRAM/DRAM ─── weights ───► MAC array
               BAD at 180nm


               ROM ASIC

  mask ROM ─── weights ───► add/sub array
               MUCH BETTER


            ROUTINGVOICE ASIC

    the physical routing IS the weights
                       │
                       ▼
                add/sub network

             NO WEIGHT FETCH

The third one is the extreme version we were discussing.

How 15 million parameters actually become silicon

Imagine one matrix has these weights:

        INPUT

        A   B   C   D

Y0      +   0   -   +
Y1      0   +   +   0
Y2      -   -   0   +

Software sees:

$$ W = \begin{bmatrix} 1&0&-1&1\\ 0&1&1&0\\ -1&-1&0&1 \end{bmatrix} $$

The ASIC compiler sees:

A ────────────────► Y0 ADD
B                       X
C ────────────────► Y0 SUB
D ────────────────► Y0 ADD


A                       X
B ────────────────► Y1 ADD
C ────────────────► Y1 ADD
D                       X


A ────────────────► Y2 SUB
B ────────────────► Y2 SUB
C                       X
D ────────────────► Y2 ADD

Therefore:

$$ Y_0=A-C+D $$ $$ Y_1=B+C $$ $$ Y_2=-A-B+D $$

There is no:

load weight
decode weight
multiply

at all.

It's just:

signal arrives
    ↓
wire carries it
    ↓
adder/subtractor

There may still be a small scaling multiplication after a block because ternary neural networks usually retain scaling factors, normalization, activation quantization, etc. BitNet itself does not literally eliminate every multiply everywhere; it makes the large matrix multiplications predominantly additions/subtractions rather than conventional weight multiplies.

Speech is particularly friendly because the input itself is slow. You're not rendering a billion pixels per second; new acoustic frames arrive on the order of milliseconds.

The one thing we cannot do

We can't naïvely make:

15,000,000 arbitrary long wires

crossing one another all over the die.

At that point the idea dies from routing congestion.

The network therefore has to be trained for the silicon architecture.

The full approach I'd use is:

Train a ~15M speech model.
Ternarize it to −1 / 0 / +1.
Encourage lots of 0s.
Impose structured sparsity, not random sparsity.
Divide every large matrix into small hardware tiles.
Constrain fan-in/fan-out.
Freeze the network.
Convert each matrix into structural RTL/netlists.
Replace +1 weights with positive routes.
Replace −1 weights with negative routes.
Delete 0 connections.
Synthesize only the adders, registers and control that remain.
Place and route it using the actual 180 nm PDK.

So the neural-network training knows something about the eventual physical topology.

That's the version I think has a chance of being genuinely beautiful.

And interestingly, this idea now has a real-world cousin

Taalas publicly unveiled essentially the same general philosophy, albeit at vastly larger scale and using its own proprietary architecture.

Taalas says it takes a trained AI model and transforms it into custom silicon, merging storage and computation instead of using a normal memory/compute boundary. Its first HC1 device hard-wires Llama 3.1 8B; Taalas reports 17,000 tokens/s/user. Their chip is 6 nm, 815 mm² and 53 billion transistors, so it is emphatically not our little edge chip — but the important part is that hard-wiring frozen models into silicon is no longer just a thought experiment.

Taalas' first silicon uses a mixture of 3-bit and 6-bit parameters, whereas RoutingVoice-180 would push the philosophy much further by combining a tiny model, ternary weights, streaming state and a very old inexpensive process.

So the one-slide explanation to your friend is basically:

GPU: "Here are 15 million numbers. Read them from memory billions of times."

RoutingVoice-180: "We already know the 15 million numbers forever. Why store and read them at all? Manufacture the answer into the wiring."

And that is the first RoutingVoice approach we were talking about.

Yes — and this is where the distinction between “weights as wiring” and “wiring is free” becomes extremely important.

For a dense 10–15M parameter network, the architecture can still eliminate weight SRAM/DRAM, but the cost moves into metal routing, fan-out, reduction trees, switching capacitance, and accumulator logic.

Inflect-Micro-v2 is a great concrete example. It has 9,356,513 complete parameters, including the waveform generator—so there is no hidden external vocoder. Its published architecture includes 96 text-hidden channels, 768 FFN channels, 192 latent channels, a 320-channel initial decoder, 3 transformer encoder layers, 4 flow blocks, and 24 kHz audio output.

2. Your accumulator won't actually be 8-bit

This is an important correction.

The input and output activations can be 8-bit.

The accumulator generally cannot.

Imagine four INT8 activations:

$$ x=[100,-20,50,7] $$

and

$$ w=[+1,-1,+1,+1] $$

Then:

$$ a= 100-(-20)+50+7 $$ $$ a=177 $$

But INT8 only goes to 127.

We haven't even summed four values yet and we've overflowed.

So the architecture is actually:

 INT8         INT8
activation    activation
    │             │
    └──────┬──────┘
           ↓
       add/subtract
           │
           ▼
     WIDE ACCUMULATOR
        15–20 bits
           │
           ▼
    scale / normalize
           │
           ▼
      requantize
           │
           ▼
          INT8
3. We can calculate accumulator width exactly

If every activation satisfies:

$$ |x|\le127 $$

and an output has fan-in \(N\),

worst case:

$$ |A_{\max}|=127N $$

Therefore the signed accumulator needs:

$$ \boxed{ B_{\mathrm{acc}} = 1+ \left\lceil \log_2(127N+1) \right\rceil } $$

So:

Dense fan-in	Worst-case sum	Accumulator
32	4,064	13 bits
64	8,128	14 bits
96	12,192	15 bits
192	24,384	16 bits
320	40,640	17 bits
576	73,152	18 bits
768	97,536	18 bits
1,024	130,048	18 bits
2,048	260,096	19 bits

Then after the dot product:

$$ q_i= \operatorname{clip}_{8} \left( \operatorname{round} \left( s_i A_i \right) \right) $$

where \(s_i\) is a learned/calibrated scale.

So perhaps:

18-bit accumulator
       │
       × scale
       │
       ▼
 round / saturate
       │
       ▼
 8-bit activation

That 8-bit value is what gets routed into the next layer.

4. Now take an Inflect-sized layer

Inflect-Micro-v2 has:

$$ d_\text{hidden}=96 $$

and:

$$ d_\text{FF}=768 $$

according to its published model specification.

Imagine, for simplicity, a dense projection:

$$ 96\rightarrow768 $$

The weight matrix is:

$$ W\in\mathbb{R}^{768\times96} $$

That's:

$$ 768\times96 = 73,728 $$

weights.

Ternarize them:

$$ W\rightarrow \{-1,+1\}^{768\times96} $$

Now we've got:

$$ \boxed{73,728\text{ fixed signed connections}} $$

but zero stored weights.

Each of the 768 output neurons receives 96 signals.

So output #37 might physically represent:

$$ y_{37} = +x_0 -x_1 +x_2 +x_3 -x_4 +\cdots -x_{95} $$

The 96 values are reduced into one result.

5. How many adders?

A straightforward binary tree adding \(N\) things needs roughly:

$$ N-1 $$

addition operations.

Therefore one 96-input output requires:

$$ 95 $$

adds/subtracts.

There are 768 outputs.

Therefore a completely spatially-unrolled implementation needs roughly:

$$ 768\times95 = 72,960 $$

adder nodes.

Just for this one projection.

Then suppose you have a reverse projection:

$$ 768\rightarrow96 $$

It contains:

$$ 96\times768 = 73,728 $$

weights again.

The reduction logic is approximately:

$$ 96\times767 = 73,632 $$

adder nodes.

So the pair is roughly:

$$ 147,456\text{ frozen weights} $$

and

$$ 146,592\text{ reduction operations} $$

if you unroll everything spatially.

This is why dense hardwired neural networks become enormous very quickly.

8. In fact, dense routing can dominate power

The basic dynamic-power equation is:

$$ P_{\rm dynamic} = \alpha C V^2 f $$

where:

\(\alpha\) = switching activity
\(C\) = switched capacitance
\(V\) = voltage
\(f\) = switching frequency.

For our fixed network:

$$ C_{\rm total} \approx \sum_{e=1}^{15M} C_e $$

So:

$$ P_{\rm routing} \approx \alpha f V^2 \sum C_e $$

This is the critical equation for RoutingVoice/ RoutingTTS.

SRAM power is gone.

But now:

$$ \boxed{\text{wire capacitance becomes the weight cost}} $$

That's a nice way to think about it.

10. And ternary math becomes ridiculously simple bit-serially

Take one activation bit plane \(b\).

Separate the connections into:

$$ P_i=\{j:w_{ij}=+1\} $$

and:

$$ N_i=\{j:w_{ij}=-1\} $$

Then for that bit:

$$ S_i^{(b)} = \sum_{j\in P_i}x_j^{(b)} - \sum_{j\in N_i}x_j^{(b)} $$

These are effectively population counts.

So hardware becomes:

                  one activation bit

                       │
            ┌──────────┴─────────┐
            │                    │

            │                    │
            ▼                    ▼
         popcount             popcount
            │                    │
            └─────── SUB ────────┘
                       │
                       ▼
                 shift by bit #
                       │
                       ▼
                wide accumulator

No INT8 multipliers.

And the fixed weight's + or - is just which reduction network the signal enters.

That's extremely attractive.

$$ 64\times64=4096 $$

All 4,096 can be frozen into wiring.

$$ 4096\times8 $$

                 64 INPUTS

rather than thousands of arbitrary die-wide wires.

That's a radically more manufacturable architecture.

13. And we can time-multiplex without bringing weight memory back

This is another important distinction.

Suppose a tile processes 64 connections per clock instead of all 4,096 simultaneously.

Normally time multiplexing implies:

“Fetch the next 64 weights.”

Not here.

We can have eight or sixteen fixed routing phases.

For example:

CLOCK 0
fixed group A
      ↓
accumulator

CLOCK 1
fixed group B
      ↓
same accumulator

CLOCK 2
fixed group C
      ↓
same accumulator

The connectivity for each phase remains compiled into logic.

No model DRAM appears.

No model SRAM appears.

We simply trade:

$$ \text{area} \leftrightarrow \text{cycles} $$

while retaining:

$$ \boxed{\text{zero weight bandwidth}} $$

That's probably the sweet spot.

17. What happens to SRAM?

Even for dense Inflect:

Weight SRAM
$$ \boxed{0} $$

potentially.

Weight DRAM
$$ \boxed{0} $$

potentially.

Activation SRAM

Still required.

For example, a 192-channel INT8 tensor containing 512 frames is:

$$ 192\times512 = 98,304\text{ bytes} $$

or approximately:

$$ \boxed{96\text{ KiB}} $$

A 320-channel tensor over the same hypothetical window would be:

$$ 320\times512 = 163,840\text{ bytes} $$

about:

$$ \boxed{160\text{ KiB}} $$

So with ping-pong buffers, scratch state, accumulators etc., you're talking hundreds of KB of working SRAM, rather than several megabytes of model weight memory.

That distinction remains extremely valuable at 180 nm.

18. The decoder is actually where I'd pay the most attention

Inflect produces the final waveform at:

$$ 24,000\text{ samples/sec}. $$

And its published upsampling stack is:

$$ 8\times8\times2\times2 = 256\times. $$

So layers near the bottom of the network execute at increasingly high temporal rates.

That means:

Even though the weights aren't being fetched, the wires and adders are switching a lot.

For TTS, the real power equation becomes:

$$ P \approx (\text{operations/second}) \times (\text{switching energy per operation}) $$

rather than merely:

$$ P\propto\text{parameter count}. $$

That's why a 9M TTS model can conceivably be more demanding than a 15M classifier running once a second.

The important correction about your current INT8 model

There are really two versions of RoutingSpeech.

Version A — freeze your current INT8 Moonshine exactly

Weights are:

$$ w_{ij}\in[-127,127]. $$

This absolutely can be hardwired.

+ wire
- wire
no wire

Instead the synthesis tool turns constant multiplication into shifts and additions.

For example:

$$ 73x = 64x+8x+x $$

or:

$$ 73x=(x\ll6)+(x\ll3)+x. $$

There is another very important Moonshine advantage

Moonshine Streaming uses bounded sliding-window attention in the encoder rather than forcing every speech frame to attend to the entire history.
